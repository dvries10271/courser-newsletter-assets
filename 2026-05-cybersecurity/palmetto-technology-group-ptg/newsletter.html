Why Cybercriminals Love Targeting SMBs — And How to Stop Them

Cyberattacks aren't just a big-business problem — and in 2026, the data makes that clearer than ever. If you run a small or mid-sized business, you're not flying under the radar. You're squarely in the crosshairs.

The threat landscape has shifted dramatically. Attackers now use AI-powered tools to scan thousands of SMB networks simultaneously, making indiscriminate, high-volume attacks the new normal. And because smaller organizations typically have fewer security controls, less staff training, and tighter budgets, they're often the easier target. The consequences are severe: 60% of small businesses that suffer a significant cyberattack close within six months.

Here's what the numbers look like right now. According to recent 2026 research, 88% of SMB breaches involved ransomware — more than double the rate seen at large enterprises. Phishing and credential theft remain the top entry points, with 80% of hacking incidents tied to compromised passwords. Perhaps most alarming: the average SMB breach can exceed $4.91 million when downtime and recovery costs are included. Yet only 20% of small businesses have implemented multi-factor authentication (MFA), and nearly half of businesses with fewer than 50 employees have zero cybersecurity budget.

So what should you do right now? CISA's guidance for small businesses is direct: enforce MFA across all accounts — especially email — keep systems patched, train employees regularly, and have a written incident response plan before you need one. These aren't advanced tactics; they're the fundamentals that most breached businesses skipped. As SonicWall's 2026 Cyber Protect Report puts it, "the vast majority of attacks... are basic fundamentals that continue to be missed."

CYBER THREAT UPDATE

Interlock Ransomware Targets Construction & Manufacturing

A CISA and FBI joint advisory highlights Interlock ransomware, which uses double extortion—encrypting data and threatening to leak it—against businesses in construction, manufacturing, and professional services. Keeping systems patched and training staff to recognize suspicious prompts are among the most effective defenses.
